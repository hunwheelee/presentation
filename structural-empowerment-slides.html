The Prevailing View
01 · The conventional wisdom

Empowering structures enable exchange.

For four decades, the field has converged on a consensus: when organizations decentralize resources, information, and sociopolitical support, employees share more, collaborate more, and adapt faster.
Structural empowerment is an enabling condition under which stable and continuous exchanges in information and resources among organizational participants are facilitated and sustained. — Mills & Ungson · AMR 2003
Echoed by Arshad et al. (2024); Evans & Davis (2005); Goedhart et al. (2017); Lee & Kim (2020); Regan et al. (2016); Sundbo (1999); and meta-analyses by Llorente-Alonso et al. (2024) and Zhang et al. (2018).

An Evolutionary Lens on Work Design
02 · Theoretical foundation

Two lenses, one root: evolutionary psychology.

I integrate two frameworks — both grounded in how human psychology evolved to navigate hierarchy, coordination, and resource scarcity — to predict who responds to empowerment with entitlement and who does not.
A

Evolutionary perspective on work design

Modern work structures pose adaptive challenges; psychological responses to those structures — including entitlement as a resource-oriented motive — were shaped by deep evolutionary history.

Nicholson 2010 · van Vugt et al. 2024 · Pitesa & Thau 2018
B

Dominance–prestige model

People pursue social rank through two distinct strategies: dominance (coercion, control) vs. prestige (earned respect). These traits shape how employees interpret empowerment cues.

Cheng & Tracy 2014 · Henrich & Gil-White 2001 · Maner & Case 2016
situational trigger × evolved individual difference → entitlement

Contributions
Theory
10 · Theoretical contributions

Three ways the field's view of empowerment should shift.

I

From uniform benefit to conditional benefit.

The dominant view — that structural empowerment uniformly facilitates exchange — misses that the same structures can reduce a positive behavior for employees high in dominance orientation.

II

A shadow path alongside the bright path.

Modeling psychological empowerment and psychological entitlement as parallel mediators reveals both an enabling process and an enshrining process. Entitlement explains unique variance beyond psychological empowerment.

III

Bringing evolved individual differences into work design.

Extending the person–situation perspective (Mischel, 1979) by incorporating evolved hierarchy-striving motives — dominance and prestige — gives a sharper account of who is susceptible to which costs of empowerment.

Practice
Implications
11 · Practical implications

Structuring empowerment with attention to who receives it.

For organizations & leaders

Don't empower blindly.

  • Selection and promotion decisions shape who carries empowerment.
  • When goal is collective exchange, pay extra attention to who will be empowered.
For empowered employees

Be self-aware of drift.

  • Watch for heightened sensitivity to disagreement or challenge.
  • Hold onto trusted peers who can flag entitled behavior early.

Future Directions
12 · The road ahead

Where the evidence should go next.

  • i. Longitudinal interventions: before/after empowerment rollouts in real organizations, observing how dynamics unfold over time.
  • ii. Cross-cultural replication, particularly in higher-power-distance contexts where empowerment cues may be construed differently.
  • iii. Further integration of evolutionary psychology with organizational design — extending the lens to other workplace structures.
